Brain Fog
Brain Fog…………………………………………….

An insoluble trick of time
We find it comic; we laugh at it.
It is a tragedy.

Brain Fog……………………………………………

An idea loses muscle, loses strength
Loses the power to stay
just one second longer,
So we can mention it, or write it.

The most fascinating thing we had to offer
at that exact moment,
that idea, that word
And that…
It's fucking gone.

Eyes paralyzed, stop.
In astonishment at what you just forgot.

Empty head.

It feels bitter
A betrayal of the brain
Your head gets heavy

A part of you is departed,
It feels like you missed the only train left.

Brain Fog…………………………………………….

That idea kidnaps your head,
You see it
You just can't mention it.

You ask for help to catch it
However,
Only you can recover it.

It reappears later
When it isn't needed anymore

You want to announce loudly
Its return,
Nobody cares
They are all trying to catch their own.

Talking is not safe anymore.

In the middle of a conversation
At the market, or with the pope
On the shore of a dark lagoon
You will be abandoned
By your own words

That language hurricane knows no hierarchies,
Respects nobody.

Why does language play that game
with you?

The game you didn't play for decades?

Hide and seek,
Becomes a regular in the relationship
With your vocabulary
With the same words you harvested.

Brain Fog……………………………………………

How can you forget a word,
An everyday word?
The one you used your whole life?

The cadence, the poetry of your voice
dissolves in the air
Just like that.

Your speech gets chopped, butchered
Sentences lose their spark

It seems to me
You are born with limited opportunities
to mention a word, an idea
at the right time.

Am I running out of that?

The drama, seriousness,
Or even the comedy of your idea
gets fogged, lost
in a momentary cloud

Grey…

Your brain is serving you drips
of resignation,
Your death is sending you
news from there,
where language is useless.

Forgotten words are amputated limbs
that you thought you would never lose.
You knew you could lose a leg or an arm,
But a word?

Oh, Fog, NO.

We gradually lose our minds, words, ideas, Language, the pavement of our lives

Brain Fog……………………………………………

But now,
I am in admiration of my own hands,
That, with drawings, come to the rescue
of the distorted road
of language at midlife.

Oh, Fog, YES

Ants

The ants are not animals, they are time.
One feels the impulse to crush them,
like you want to crush time.

One contemplates them pass,
one by one, like you contemplate time.
Ants are silent and effective;
they appear and disappear as time does.

You know exactly when an ant is walking on your skin.
Your body knows exactly how it feels,
how to assess its weight, its speed.

A massive rainfall and a colony of ants
can arouse the same sensations in your brain.
You stop and wait for them to pass.

One wishes to climb walls the way they do.
The wind against the wall doesn't blow the ants.
Seconds, either, can't be blown from life.
Seconds never stop, like there is no such thing as an ant stopping.
Their life is a second long,
their body is a second long.

Ants autogenerate next to delicious sweet things,
the evidence that pleasure and time made pacts.

Ants are golden, like time.

Embroideries Project

The embroideries project is a quest to dissect what it means to wait, what it means to look at longing in the eyes and have an honest conversation with it.

Is waiting an island, or a void?
How do you give wings to a time spent waiting?

The strongest image I have in answer to this question is my grandmother, embroidering. She surrendered the weight of losing her daughter to the magic of entangling her fingers in thread and needle. She created the space for the language of dreams to land in front of our eyes. She, with a group of 40 women (el costurero), in the afternoons, decoded the myths of existence one stitch at a time.

This project is a homage to Azucena, to her lessons on creative waiting, she showed me the intangible, still colossal value of applying one's life to the act of creating.

For this project, I am collaborating with a group of women in Medellín, led by Angela María Arango, who, like my grandmother, loves to embroider. I lived in Angela's apartment as a child, in Sabaneta, a municipality in the Medellín metropolitan area. In the house she lived with her three daughters, her husband, and her mother. We were many, but she always made sure I had all the love I needed while my mother was away working. She has known me since I was four years old.

Angela has brought the same joy, excellence and care to my drawings that she brought to the days and months I spent in her house.

It is the objective of this project to include women who understand the power of creative waiting, like Azucena did.

Her silence while embroidering lives in my hands.

Aire y Abrigo

Aire y Abrigo

In my work, I find air and shelter when I walk the fine line between the animal and the human: innate contrasts in the experience of growing up in a place of natural beauty and excruciating violence, exquisite storytelling and humor.

In every piece I return to the condition of being an animal, a discrete piece of flesh that can still hold profound connections with other bodies and the unknown.

Portraying semi-abstract figures gave me a solid ground to emancipate from and a place to return to: air and shelter.

Embroideries

An homage to my grandmother's body, my first nation, my land. Knowing her body from so many angles gave me the feeling that I can paint it from the inside.

Her body moved through the house in the form of fog in the mornings, breeze on hot afternoons, and warmth on cold nights. That body was both air and shelter.

Worked in black thread on unbleached linen, in these embroideries the line becomes the primary tool of anatomy used to portray her body, both human and animal. Dense vertical hatching builds fields of fur or falling water, while radiating and coiled stitches mark sites of concentration within the form, organ-like or seed-like masses held within the larger silhouette.

Contour is drawn in fine running and backstitch; the raw linen remains bare skin, breath, or void. Stitch density and direction do the work of shading and volume, so that the body is not illustrated but built, thread by thread, the way flesh accumulates over bone.

Paintings

In these paintings, I depict a series of imaginary encounters. A personal story of longing is encoded in the playfulness of the intertwined figures.

The pieces have their genesis in saturated drawings, fields of geometric patterns and instinctual gestures, the process of finding the soul of each composition became a game of subtraction and sculpting: these paintings often demanded a strenuous physicality for the precision of forms and the exercise of restraint, which I normally don't do when drafting or drawing.

The semi-abstract animals emerge in black and white blocks against raw grounds with weight and character, as if they existed before the painting began, and found in my hands only a vehicle into existence. The figures aim their energy outside the canvas to evoke the tension of longing for an encounter.

I am interested in the condition of being an animal,
a discrete piece of flesh that can still hold profound connections with other bodies and the unknown.

Bio

Ana Lucía Cano (b. 1980, Armenia, Quindío, Colombia) is an artist working across painting, embroidery, and print.

Cano studied architecture in Medellín before moving to Montreal to complete her studies at McGill University. After graduating, she worked in Bogotá for Daniel Bonilla Arquitectos and Giancarlo Mazzanti, developing a series of international competitions. Subsequently, she worked as an architect in Vienna for two and a half years, developing residential and high-rise projects.

In 2007, Cano moved to New York City to pursue a Master's degree in Architectural Design at Columbia University. After completing her studies, she worked on the design and planning of the New York Police Academy in Queens. After this, she joined Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM), where she participated in the design of high-rise buildings in Mexico City and the Baccarat Hotel in midtown Manhattan. She is a licensed architect in the State of New York.

Cano is a self-taught artist. In 2015, she felt a strong calling and began drawing original black and white pieces, which became the foundation of her work and her visual language. Following the birth of her son in 2018, she and her family moved to Barcelona, where she based her studio and continues her journey as an artist. Cano's work has been acquired for private collections in India, Spain, England, the United States, Colombia, and Mexico. She currently lives and works in Barcelona.

Ana

Cano's childhood was split between a city at war, Medellín, and the expansive life of her grandmother's farm; between the discipline of religious school and barefoot days among coffee plantations.

After a successful career as an architect and several years of psychoanalysis, Ana had the realization that the relationship between head and hand was an enormous, dormant part of her. Stories surfaced, and insisted on being told, both in images and in words. Being an artist became a way to tell those stories and to reconcile with the tumult of her early years.

Each project is built layer by layer, stitch by stitch, from the specific: her grandmother, a family of butchers, a close relationship with animals on the farm, the afternoons in the stable, a mother away working in remote regions of Colombia, raised by several caretakers.

Cano works across painting, embroidery, and print, but the instinct beneath each is the same: the condition of being an animal, a discrete piece of flesh that can still hold profound connections with other bodies and the unknown.

She begins with wireframe sketches that serve as both a map and a narrative outline. Patterns, gestural lines, and subtle washes are woven together over time, allowing details to emerge from an innate structure.

Her semi-abstract work blends the precision of architectural draftsmanship with the freedom of intuitive mark-making. Figures, animals and shapes are rendered in gestures that are familiar enough to recognize, yet strange enough to unsettle, inviting the viewer to become aware of their own emotions.
